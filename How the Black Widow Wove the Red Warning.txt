Listen closely and listen well, O Little Night-Wanderer. For this happened back in the Very-Near-Now. Right on the rough and ragged edge of the Sprawling Suburbs.

In those days, she had no Night-black Coat at all.

No Red-marked Warning.

The small and silent Dust-brown Spider.

She had a fierce, forgiving heart, but a soft, small, easily-squished shape. She wished to keep her babies completely safe. But she could not run. She could not roar. She could not fly. She only possessed her corners.

Her deep, dark, under-the-flowerpot corners.

Now, you must understand this, O Little Night-Wanderer. The Tall Two-Legs did not look in the under-places. They marched across the bright, wide patios. And they swept away the dust.

They swept it with the Great Sweep-swish Broom.

They swung it by the handle with a swish-shhh-swish, and left no shadow undisturbed.

Beneath the cracked clay flowerpot, she spun her hidden home.

The small and silent Dust-brown Spider.

Not a shining, silver circle. Not a pretty, perfect wheel. She spun a cross-and-crinkle, kink-and-knot, trip-and-tangle web.

And deep inside the tangles, she kept her little ones.

Three soft, pale, round egg-sacs.

Her precious, silk-spun moon-seeds.

She wrapped them and she wove them. She guarded and she grieved. She wanted nothing more than to wait in the dark until they hatched.

But before the moon could rise, the Back Door slid open.

Shruck-shrick-shrack!

Out came the Tall Two-Legs.

Out came the Great Sweep-swish Broom.

Swish-shhh-swish!

It swept the leaves. It swept the dirt. It swept right under the cracked clay flowerpot!

Silk-snap!

One thread broke.

The web shook.

The first egg-sac trembled in its cradle.

She ran out from the dark, all eight feet at once. She plucked the strings to say, “Stay away.”

Tremble-tick.

Tremble-tick.

But brooms are very bad at listening.

Swish-shhh-swish!

The Broom struck the web. Down fell one pale egg-sac into the dust.

She rushed forward and bit the stiff straw bristles.

But biting a broom is like scolding a stone.

The Broom did not feel it.

The Broom did not care.

She was completely defeated.

She gathered up her fallen egg-sac, patched what was torn, and hid in the deep, dark dust.

High on the patio wall, a quick shadow looked down.

The Fence-line Lizard with the flick-a-flick tongue.

Flick-a-flick!

“You are too small to fight,” he hissed and he hummed. “A bite is just a bother if they cannot see the teeth. You cannot stop the sweeping if you do not have a sign.”

“But I have no sign,” she whispered.

The small and silent Dust-brown Spider.

“Then you must steal one,” he answered.

The Fence-line Lizard with the flick-a-flick tongue.

“Look to the edge of the street. Look to the Great Red Street-Leaf. It stands on one metal leg. It has no teeth. It has no claws. But when the Roaring Metal Monsters see its bright red face, they always, always stop.”

So she crept and she crawled. She scrambled and scurried. She ran past the fence. She ran past the Great Green Grumbling Plastic Bins. She ran all the way to the edge of the roaring road.

She looked up at the towering, terrifying metal pole.

The Great Red Street-Leaf.

She climbed and she climbed. She hoisted and hauled.

Up past the dust.

Up past the rust.

Right up to the bright red face of the sign.

She reached out with her small, soft feet. She stole one single thread of stopping. One single string of warning red. She tucked it tightly beneath her belly, and she carried it all the way home.

By the time she reached the cracked clay flowerpot, the moon was up, white and wide and watching.

She climbed into her broken web, and she began to weave.

The small and silent Dust-brown Spider.

She wove the red through the shadow. She wove the red through the fear. She wove the red through the memory of the Broom.

She wove and she wove, under and over.

But she did not weave a circle.

She did not weave a square.

She wove the red thread into an hourglass.

Because what the small and silent mother wanted was time.

Time to be seen.

Time to be spared.

And as she finished her weaving, the magic washed over her. It washed away the brownish and the dustish and the dullish. She became as black as the back of the night.

So the red would burn bright.

When morning came, the Tall Two-Legs stepped out onto the patio. They reached down with their thick, heavy gardening gloves.

The Five-Fingered Hide-Hand.

Scrape-scuff-reach.

The hand reached under the cracked clay flowerpot.

She did not run.

She did not bite.

She hung upside down beneath her precious, silk-spun moon-seeds.

She plucked the web.

Tremble-tick.

Tremble-tick.

The hand moved closer in the dark.

And then, the Tall Two-Legs saw it.

Burning bright against the Night-black Coat.

The hourglass of warning.

The little red sign of stopping.

The hand froze.

The hand backed away.

The web was completely safe.

And that is the reason, to this very minute, that she hangs upside down in the dark.

The patient and poisonous Red-marked Widow.

It shows she belongs to the under-places. And you will notice she never weaves a shining circle. And she wears the little red stop-sign beneath her body, just to make strictly sure that careless hands are given time.

To see the warning.

To back away.

And leave the web alone.

With that burning, bright red, hourglass mark.

So, the very next time you lie in your bed in the dark, O Little Night-Wanderer, pull the heavy blankets right up to your chin.

Close your heavy, sleepy eyes.

Listen past the streetlamps.

Listen past the roaring roads.

And if you are very, very still…

You can hear the under-places settling down to sleep.

Down in the dark corners.

Down in the quiet dust.

Where the patient mothers are rocking their silk-spun moon-seeds.

Keeping them safe.

Keeping them still.

Tremble-tick.

Tremble-tick.

Tremble-tick.

Fast asleep.
